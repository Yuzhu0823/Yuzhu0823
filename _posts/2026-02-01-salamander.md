---
title: "A single cell becoming a salamander"
---

So sam showed me a time-lapse "https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SEejivHRIbE" of a single cell becoming a salamandar like two months ago, and I've been haunted by that video.

The first two splits of the initial cell looked normal enough. It looked like someone was trying to divide a cake into four equal pieces by cutting through the middle once and adding an orthogonal cut. Then it got weird. The next split cuts a circle around half the radius of the cake, dividing the original 4 cells to 8. That split looked asymmetrical in the sense that the 4 cells in the inner layer have very different shape from the 4 cells in the outer layer. And in hindsight, no split was ever symmetrical. The first split didn't yield two cells of the exact same size, neither did the second split. Also by "symmetrical", I'm only talking about the appearance, i.e. the size and shape of the cell. But as sam pointed out, there is a lot going on underneath that I couldn't see, and the asymmetry was present in the very first cell. Even though from the video the initial cell seems like a homogeneous whole.

Another thing that strikes me about the development going on in the egg is that it's completely automated. The developing egg looked like a closed system. After the sperm entry, there wasn't any external information going into the egg. The fact that it kept dividing, changing shapes, evovling without any external directions was wild. The confusion comes from the scale of perspective. Viewing the egg as a whole and seeing it shift shape didn't make sense. But looking at it as a group of cells each of which as an agent that acts under a set of rules governed by physics and chemistry made sense. The paradigm is captured by cornway's game of life where the state of each cell at the next time point is determined by the state of its 8 neighboring cells. Cornway's game of life is a zero-player game, the way it progresses is completely decided by the way it's initialized (and importantly the rules), and no further input is required. This is the exact model for embryogenesis. But the reason embryogensis is weird to watch is the "rules" each cell follows are hidden. As a computer science graduate, I'm very comfortable with the concept of automation. I mean, running any program is simply feeding the input to a defined algorithm and getting the output. It was easy to understand when the "rules" are just codes, hand-crafted by man, simple. But when it's crafted by nature and evolution, so many molecules at play according to the rule of physics and chemistry, but so little is uncovered, the rules seem "hidden". This is true for other self-organizing systems as well. The swarming behavior of birds and fish is similar. From the whole, it looks like a cloud of points changing shapes to avoid attacks from a predator, it looks as if the swarm, as a whole, is intelligent. But zooming in, it's each bird following the same set of rules embedded in the brain of this species, deciding exactly how to move based on its neighboring birds. 

Embryogenesis of a ovipartity animal is still wilder than swarm intelligence, in the sense that I see why the swarms need to shapeshift, to move. The system(the swarm) is constantly receiving external input, e.g. signal of food, signal of predator, and reacting accordingly. When I think about embryogenesis, the oviparity kind to be exact, the egg is a closed system, not receiving information from the outside other than temperature, gravity, so what is it that prompts all the development inside. If all conditions remain the same, why would anything change. There are fertilized eggs that just stay eggs instead of becoming chicken, so I guess the hen needs to hatch it to get the process started. Even the condition that prompts the start of the gestation being satisfied is the change, e.g. hen hatching them, things are stable during the gestation, i.e.temperature stays the same, gravitational field pointing at the same direction, then what prompts the continuous differentiation. Why would things change when there is no changing input? From an information point of view, this could be maybe be explained as: once a program(embryogenesis) starts, as long as the conditions that keep the program running is still valid, the program would just run till it finishes. But what is finishing anyway, the chick hatches, it came out of the isolated system, matures, reproduces, and dies, then the body rots, decomposes and feed the soil that grows plants. Zooming in to molecular level, there is no such thing as a "program", there is no intention, just molecules reacting according to the laws of nature. So from the perspective of molecules and what prompts the interactions among them, I guess it's them not being at the most stable state. This also seems counterintuitive, because as the embryogenesis process goes along, the structure becomes more and more complex, the cells become less and less similar to each other, it seems like the entropy/energy is building up instead of releasing. Intuitively, the energy of the enclosed egg is building up. The development of the opposite, i.e. the process of dying and rotting, seems more natural then this. My bf gave me an analogy when we were talking about this, he said is it weird to you that a winded toy car can go up the hills? There is nothing natural about defying gravity, but with enough energy stored, pointing at an uphill direction, the toy car can achieve the unnatural.
