---
title: "Diffusion models and the manifold of images"
---

Diffusion model has puzzled me in the sense that I don't see how information can be generated from noise. I guess in that sense, generative models in general puzzle me. I understand that it's about characterizing the data distribution $$p(X)$$ where the real world images(or whatever data that's being modeled) are drawn so that we can arbitrarily sample from it to generate new images. The distribution of real world images $$p(X)$$, where say $$X\in R^{3 \times 1080 \times 762}$$ is hard to think about. A 1d distribution is easy to visualize as a histogram, a 2d distribution is still easy to visualize as a contour map over the plane. When the dimension keeps increasing, we are running out of ways to visualize it and thus understand it intuitively. The way I ended up thinking about the real world images' distribution in a $$3 \times 1080 \times 762$$ high dimensional space is this: the space itself is vast, the majority points in the space makes no sense to human perception(not a meaningful image, just noise). The images lie on a low-dimensional manifold within the high-dimensional space, and what defines that manifold is a set of constraints that make images meaningful to us. For example, adjacent pixels, for the most part, need to have similar color values, so that patches can form. Without this constraint, the image would most likely look like noise. This constraint itself binds variables $$x_{i,j}$$ with 8 other variables in its neighborhood at least: $$x_{i-1,j-1}, x_{i,j-1}, x_{i+1,j-1}, x_{i-1,j},x_{i+1, j},x_{i+1,j+1},x_{i+1,j},x_{i+1,j+1}$$, and this happens for all $$1080 \times 762$$ pixels. In reality, for meaningful patches to form, the binding of variables should spread even further out than each pixels' immediate neighbor.

We went to the archibald prize exhibition in NSW art gallery the other day, and there was a painting that looks much like this one: https://www.newyorker.com/culture/photo-booth/an-alluring-history-of-photographic-blur. We can tell it's a blurry image of a woman. It struck me how few color patches are needed to visually define something. It's the triangular shade and the highlighting patch next to it that defined the pointy nose, the rosy burgundy patch with shade in between and underneath that defined the mouth, the pinkish grayish diffusive shade that defined the chin. It's these elements together that collectively convinced me it's a face. All this is to say: If we are only talking face, and only asking for images that us human brains could perceive as face, the bare minimum seems to be lying on a VERY LOW dimensional manifold. There seems to be a spectrum, on the one end, there are these semantic defining, low-frequency, low resolution images which gives us the most information of what the image is with very few color patches. And as we move to the other end, smaller color patches appear, and we would get more detailed but less important information such as the clear boundary of the eyes, nose, jaw, even the texture of the skin when we move all the way to high resolution. If we run PCA and only take the first few principal components to reconstruct the image, the image would land on the low-resolution end of the spectrum. The same with fourier discrete transformation, if we only use low frequency waves, the image would be approximated as something on the low-resolution end. It seems like the low frequency patches explains the most variation within the image and tell us the most. I wonder what the relationship between low and high resolution version of the same image is in the space? Is it the same manifold? Or does low resolution images lie in one manifold, and high resolution images lie in another manifold? Because it seems like there is different levels of constraints in the two version.

By now, it has become clear that it's very hard to describe $$p(X)$$. We understand there are constraints among variables, but can't explicitly write those constraints down. The way GAN deals with it is to avoid explicitly define $$p(X)$$ as a distribution family with parameters to learn, rather it uses discriminator to decide whether a given sample satisfies the constraints it has seen in training images or not. VAE and diffusion model, on the other hand, try to write $$p(X)$$ down explicitly with variational inference. With VAE, $$log\ p(X) = log\int p(x,z)dz = log\int p(z)p(x \mid z)dz = log\int q(z \mid x)\frac{p(z)p(x \mid z)}{q(z \mid x)}dz\geq \sum_{z\sim q(z \mid x)}log\frac{p(z)p(x \mid z)}{q(z \mid x)} = -D_{KL}(q(z \mid x) \Vert p(z)) + \sum_{z\sim q(z \mid x)}log\ p(x \mid z)$$ which gives us the ELBO with the KL divergence part and the reconstruction part. In the VAE formulation, the latent variable $$z$$ prior is a multivariate gaussian distribution , and the conditional probability $$p(x \mid z)$$ is also assumed to be gaussian $$p(x \mid z)\sim N(\mu_\phi(z),I)$$. The assumption is too simple for real world images, and VAE suffers from blurry images. Diffusion model assumes a chain of gaussian transformation from gaussian noise $$x_T\sim N(0,I)$$, $$x_{t-1}\sim N(\mu_\theta(x_t, t),\Sigma(x_t, t))$$ till $$x_0$$ is reconstructed. This is a discretization of the continuous-time diffusion theoretical framework, where the diffusion process is characterized by $$dx = f(x,t)dt + dW$$. This, given time step $$t$$, defines a field that for each $$x$$ in the field, we know where it's going next with a bit of randomness added $$dW$$. I like to think of the diffusion process of images as points on the image manifold, going out towards other places in the ambient space outside the manifold, eventually centered at $$\vec{0}$$. The field is fluid through time. 

To think intuitively about what diffusion model is trying to learn. The forward SDE $$dx = -\frac{\beta(t)}{2}xdt + \sqrt{\beta(t)}dW$$. At each time point, the deterministic part $$-\frac{\beta(t)}{2}xdt $$ scales $$x$$ down at the time and drift it to the origin, and the stochastic part $$\sqrt{\beta(t)}dW$$ provides a jitter around the deterministic part. Intuitively, each data point goes from the data manifold, zig-zagging it's way towards some point around origin. The whole point is to learn the reverse of the forward SDE< so that we can go from sampled noise back to somewhere in the data manifold to generate realistic pictures. The reverse is $$dx = (-\frac{\beta(t)}{2}x - \beta(t)\nabla_xlogp_t(x))dt + \sqrt{\beta(t)}dW$$. The new part $$\nabla_xlogp_t(x)dt$$ requires knowing the data density over the space at time $$t$$. Is this known? As each points go through this forward SDE, the distribution of drifted points over the space at any time point $$t$$, $$p_t(x_t)$$, is fully determined $$p_t(x_t) = \int{p(x_t \mid x_0)p(x_0)dx_0}$$. Point $$x_t$$ at time $$t$$ could have been drifted from any sample taken from the data distribution, more likely from some samples, less likely from others, so the probability is a integral over all samples. 

The reverse process is basically saying, at each time $$t$$, move $$x$$ towards the direction where the distribution of data at diffusion time $$t$$ $$p_t(x)$$,which is determined by the forward pass, is denser. Starting out from $$x_T \sim N(0,I)$$, the distribution $$p_T(x)$$ is centered around origin, but denser on the locations where it's more likely to have been diffused from all possible $$x_{T-1}$$ integrated. Now we look to the distribution of $$p_{T-1}(x)$$, it would be further from the origin, and denser in locations more likely to have been diffused from all $$x_{T-2}$$. As $$x$$ is moving towards a direction where the previous timestep would likely land at each move, it's slowly being sucked back to the data manifold. Each timestep has its own manifold, the data is traveling backwards through each manifold, and landing in the last manifold which is the original data manifold. Maybe manifold approaching time $$T$$ corresponds to manifold of images reconstructed with few PCs, or low frequency fourier component, where the main structure is still there but the fine details are gone.
